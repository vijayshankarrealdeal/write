The best writing software of 2026.

Eight long-form writing tools, picked for novelists and serious drafters. We've used each one on a real manuscript for at least a week. No affiliate links, no marketing fluff — just what holds up when you sit down to write 90,000 words.

Updated May 2026 · 14 min read · Includes free + paid options

How we picked

"Best" depends on what you're writing. We narrowed the field to tools that meet four bars:

  1. Long-form first. Hierarchical structure (chapters & scenes), not a single textarea or a notes app.
  2. Reliable autosave + sync. Local cache + cross-device. Losing a session is disqualifying.
  3. Active development. Updates within the last six months.
  4. Honest pricing. No surprise upgrades, no per-feature paywalls hidden behind a 30-day trial.

Tools that flunk those (we tried plenty): Pages, Word, Google Docs alone, plain text editors, several of the small "AI book writer" startups. They have their place — they're just not best-in-class for novel-length work.

Quick verdict

  • Best overall (free): InkSpaceX — long-form structure, native apps on every OS, free.
  • Best paid for desktop-only macOS workflow: Scrivener 3 — the Compile dialog is unmatched.
  • Best for AI-first generative drafting: Sudowrite — designed around the brainstorm + cards + canvas loop.
  • Best for academic / nonfiction with citations: Scrivener (footnotes & references) or InkSpaceX (footnotes + endnotes on publish) — both beat the rest.
  • Best for screenwriters: Final Draft / Highland 2 — none of the prose tools below are screenplay-formatted.
  • Best for collaborative drafting: Google Docs (with caveats) — real-time co-editing remains its superpower.
  • Avoid: tools that only work online, only on one OS, or that lock your manuscript inside a proprietary cloud with no .docx export.

The eight tools

1. InkSpaceX — our pick for free

InkSpaceX is the modern long-form writing tool we wish had existed when we started. Hierarchical binder (Acts → Chapters → Scenes), three views (⌘1 Draft, ⌘2 Cards, ⌘3 Outline), Scrivenings continuous mode (⌘4), Page View (⌘5), per-section synopsis + notes + keywords, footnotes with endnote rendering on publish, author bookmarks, snapshots, Linguistic Focus (highlight by part of speech), AI co-writer (Pro, opt-in), real-time cross-device sync, and a built-in reader so finished work has somewhere to land. Free tier covers all of it; Pro is $8/mo for AI + audio chapters + custom domain.

Best for: writers who switch devices, want a free tier that isn't a clock, want optional AI without it being the centerpiece, or care about their manuscript reaching readers.

2. Scrivener 3

The veteran. Twenty years of refinement, a Binder + Inspector model that influenced everything else, and a Compile dialog that no competitor has matched for print-ready output. Limitations show up off the desktop: no first-party Android app, no in-browser editor, Dropbox sync model that requires "close before switching." One-time license per OS (~$60 each), no real free tier.

Best for: 100% Mac/Windows desktop writers shipping print books with complex layouts.

3. Sudowrite

AI-first writing tool. Generative cards (Write, Brainstorm, Describe, Twist, Canvas) wrapped in a writer's chrome. Excellent for blank-page rescues and divergent ideation. Less of a long-form project manager — there's no real binder, no Scrivenings, no Page View, no footnotes. Web-only. Pricing is credit-based; expect ~$19–$29/month for serious use.

Best for: writers whose blocker is the blank page rather than structure.

4. Ulysses

Markdown-native long-form editor. Beautiful typography, library + groups + sheets organisation, iCloud sync. Best on Apple platforms; no Windows or Android app. Subscription only (~$6/month or $40/year). Excellent for nonfiction and blogging; lighter on novel-specific features (no Cards/Outline view, no Scrivenings, no Linguistic Focus).

Best for: Apple-only Markdown loyalists writing essays, articles, or shorter fiction.

5. Novlr

Web-first novel writing tool. Has a binder, basic AI assist, daily streaks, and a community. The editor is solid; the project structure is Scrivener-lite. Subscription only (~$9/month). Owned and run by writers, which shows in priorities.

Best for: writers who want a cozy web-first community alongside the tool.

6. Plottr

Not a drafting tool — a story-structure visualiser. Beat sheets, character arcs, timelines. You write elsewhere; you plan in Plottr. Pairs well with any of the editors above. One-time purchase or subscription.

Best for: outliners and plotters who treat planning as a separate phase.

7. Google Docs

Free, ubiquitous, real-time collaborative. Lacks a binder, Cards view, Scrivenings, snapshots scoped to scenes, and footnotes that survive export to ePub well. Many writers use it as the universal "share with my editor" layer rather than the primary draft.

Best for: co-writing with another human in the same document; sharing a chapter for feedback.

8. Microsoft Word

Word still has the most powerful single-document editor of any tool here. Pair it with a strong outline + headings discipline and you can ship a novel from it. No project structure, no Scrivenings, no Cards view, no built-in publish path. Best as the final compile target rather than the daily drafting tool.

Best for: writers whose publisher requires Word and who don't want to round-trip through Compile.

What we deliberately left out

Three categories didn't make this list and you might wonder why:

  • Notion / Obsidian / Roam. Excellent thinking tools, painful long-form drafting tools. No Compile, no manuscript view, no scene-level versioning. If you world-build there and write in something else, that's a great workflow — but the writing software itself isn't here.
  • "AI book in 5 minutes" startups. They make demos, not books. None of the seven we tried produced a draft a working writer would publish. Skip.
  • Final Draft, Highland 2, WriterDuet. Great for screenwriting; outside this list's scope (prose long-form). If you're writing a screenplay, those three are where to look.

How to choose in five questions

  1. Where do I write? One desktop only → Scrivener / Ulysses. Multiple devices → InkSpaceX / Google Docs.
  2. What's my blocker? Blank page → Sudowrite. Structure → InkSpaceX / Scrivener. Both → InkSpaceX (binder + AI).
  3. Do I need footnotes? Yes → InkSpaceX or Scrivener. No → any.
  4. What's my budget? $0 → InkSpaceX. ~$60 once → Scrivener. ~$10/mo → Novlr / Ulysses / InkSpaceX Pro. ~$25+/mo → Sudowrite.
  5. What happens when I'm done? Publish to readers → InkSpaceX. Send to my editor in Word → Word / Docs. Export to print → Scrivener.
