Outline a novel with the
Three-Act Structure (free template).

The Three-Act Structure isn't a formula. It's a load-bearing skeleton that holds about 95% of working commercial fiction. Here's what each act has to do, the seven turning points that hold the whole shape together, and a binder template you can drop into any writing app to start tomorrow.

Published May 6, 2026 · 12 min read · The InkSpaceX Editors

If you read interviews with novelists, you'll notice something strange. Half of them swear by the Three-Act Structure and half claim never to use any structure at all. Both groups are partly bluffing. The first group is selling a method; the second group is selling intuition. The truth is duller and more useful: most working fiction sits inside something that looks like Three-Act Structure, whether the writer planned it that way or backed into it through revision.

Calling it a "structure" sets up the wrong expectation, though. It is not a template you fill in like a tax form. It is a description of how readers experience a story that holds together. If you understand what each act is doing for the reader, you can outline freely inside it without the result feeling formulaic. That's the whole bet of this post.

What the three acts actually do

Forget percentages and word counts for a minute. The three acts each have one job:

  • Act I — Setup. Make the reader care. Introduce the protagonist's world, give us a reason to invest in them, and disturb that world so badly that returning to normal is no longer possible.
  • Act II — Confrontation. Make the reader uncertain. The protagonist tries to solve the problem and keeps making it worse. Rising stakes, narrowing options, deepening understanding of who they actually are.
  • Act III — Resolution. Make the reader pay off. The protagonist faces what they've been avoiding and either becomes the person who can resolve it or breaks trying. Either way, the world that opens at the end is fundamentally different from the one that opened at the start.

That's it. Setup, confrontation, resolution. Aristotle was talking about Greek drama 2,400 years ago and he had it.

What changes between writers — and what makes a story feel fresh or rote — is the seven turning points that connect those acts.

The seven turning points

Every Three-Act story rests on seven moments. If any of them are missing, the act they belong to feels structurally off, even if the prose is beautiful. If you put them in approximately the right places, the story feels like it knows where it's going, even when you're improvising the scenes.

1. Hook (opening pages)

An image, a question, a voice — something that makes the reader want to spend ninety thousand words with this narrator. Not a plot event. A tone. Tartt's The Secret History opens with a confession of a murder that won't pay off for two hundred pages. Beloved opens with a sentence about a haunted house. The job is to set the temperature.

2. Inciting incident (around page 12)

The thing that yanks the protagonist out of their normal life. Hagrid arrives with a letter. The ring lands in Frodo's lap. The body is found. Until this happens, you don't have a story — you have a setup. The inciting incident does not have to be loud, but it has to be irreversible. The protagonist cannot pretend they didn't see it.

3. First plot point (end of Act I, around 25%)

The protagonist commits. They cross a threshold from "this thing happened to me" to "I am now actively pursuing/fleeing/confronting it." This is where the story you sold the reader in your query letter actually begins. Frodo leaves the Shire. Katniss volunteers as Tribute. The detective takes the case.

4. Midpoint (the middle of Act II, around 50%)

The protagonist learns something that changes the game — about themselves, about the antagonist, about the nature of the conflict. The midpoint divides Act II into a "reaction" half and an "action" half. Before the midpoint, things happen to the protagonist. After it, the protagonist starts driving. If your novel sags in the middle, you almost certainly have a midpoint problem.

5. Second plot point (end of Act II, around 75%)

The all-is-lost moment. The protagonist's plan has failed. The mentor is dead, the antagonist has won the round, the love interest has walked out, the climbing rope has snapped. This is the dark night of the soul, and it has to be earned by everything that came before. Don't fake the despair.

6. Climax (Act III, around 90%)

The confrontation the whole novel has been pointing at. The protagonist either becomes the version of themselves that can win, or they fail. Notice that "becoming the version of themselves" is more important than "winning" — winning without internal change reads as cheap. The climax is the externalisation of the protagonist's arc.

7. Resolution (last few pages)

What does the world look like now? Show the new normal. Show what the protagonist's choice cost. Resolution doesn't mean tidy — it means honest. The Road resolves; nothing is fixed. Pride and Prejudice resolves; everyone gets married. The job is to show the reader that the world has come to rest.

How long is each act?

For a 90,000-word novel, the rough division is:

  • Act I: 0–22,500 words (25%)
  • Act II: 22,500–67,500 words (50%)
  • Act III: 67,500–90,000 words (25%)

These are not laws. They are the medians of working commercial fiction across decades. You can absolutely write a literary novel where Act I runs to 35% and the climax is folded into the resolution. But if your Act I is 50% of your manuscript, you don't have a structural problem — you have a pacing problem. Readers feel it.

One more useful rule: Act II should always be the longest, and the midpoint should fall around the literal middle of the manuscript. If you are 60% through your draft and you can't identify a midpoint, your story isn't actually a story yet.

The free binder template

Here is the structure we use ourselves. Drop these chapters into your writing app's binder, paste in any notes you have, and you have an outline you can write against. We've kept the names neutral so the template works for any genre.

Manuscript
├── Act I — Setup
│ ├── Chapter 1: Hook + ordinary world
│ ├── Chapter 2: Show what's at stake (relationships, ambition)
│ ├── Chapter 3: Inciting incident
│ ├── Chapter 4: Refusal / first response
│ └── Chapter 5: First plot point — protagonist commits
├── Act II.A — Reaction
│ ├── Chapter 6: New world, new rules
│ ├── Chapter 7: First obstacle
│ ├── Chapter 8: Allies + complications
│ ├── Chapter 9: Pinch point — antagonist's first real move
│ └── Chapter 10: Reckoning with the cost
├── Act II.B — Action
│ ├── Chapter 11: Midpoint — the realisation
│ ├── Chapter 12: Counter-attack
│ ├── Chapter 13: False victory
│ ├── Chapter 14: Second pinch point
│ └── Chapter 15: Second plot point — all is lost
├── Act III — Resolution
│ ├── Chapter 16: Regrouping
│ ├── Chapter 17: Final approach
│ ├── Chapter 18: Climax
│ └── Chapter 19: Resolution / new normal

Each chapter line is a placeholder. Some chapters in your draft will end up being two or three; some will collapse into one. The point of the template isn't to constrain you — it's to give you a shape you can argue with.

How to actually use it

Open your writing app, create the binder folders, and write a one-sentence synopsis in each chapter's notes. Don't worry about prose yet. Just answer: What changes for the protagonist in this chapter?

If you can answer that for all nineteen, you have an outline. If you can't answer it for several adjacent chapters, you have found the part of your story that isn't yet a story. That is the most valuable thing this template does for you.

Some writers expand each synopsis into a paragraph before drafting. Some prefer a single sentence. The point isn't the level of detail — it's that you can see the whole shape on one screen before you commit to scenes.

What about pantsers?

If you've made it this far thinking "I'd never plot like this," good — you might still find this useful. Pantsers (writers who discover the story by writing it) often produce first drafts where Act I bleeds into Act II for thirty thousand words because nothing has irreversibly happened yet. They land the inciting incident at chapter twelve and the first plot point at chapter twenty.

Knowing the structure is what lets a pantser revise efficiently. You do not need to outline before drafting; you do need to know what an outline looks like so you can recognise the bones inside what you wrote and reshape them.

The thing nobody tells you

The Three-Act Structure is a description of experience, not a recipe for storytelling. When a story works, readers feel a setup, a confrontation, and a resolution — even if the writer never thought in those terms. When a story doesn't work, the reader can usually point to which act felt thin without having a vocabulary for it.

That's why the template above has nineteen chapters and not twenty-seven beats with cute names. The nineteen-chapter shape is the floor: the minimum geometry that produces the experience. Everything else is your story.

Now go outline.
