Why your first draft should be ugly: a working writer's guide to fast drafting.

Slow first drafts kill more novels than bad ideas do. The writers who finish books, year after year, share one habit: they get a deliberately rough complete draft on the page fast — eight to twelve weeks for a full novel — and then revise from a finished thing instead of polishing a fragment.

May 6, 2026 · 11 min read · Pairs with the three-act outline

I have a folder on my hard drive labelled "abandoned." Twenty-seven novels live there. Each one made it past chapter three, none made it past chapter ten. The pattern is identical: I started strong, I started rewriting chapter one before chapter five existed, and by chapter ten I no longer believed in the version of the story that chapter one promised. So I quit.

I have a different folder labelled "finished." Five novels live there. The thing they share isn't talent or time or lucky timing — those are wildly inconsistent. The thing they share is that I wrote each one ugly first, on purpose, and didn't allow myself to revise until the end was on the page.

This post is about why that works, what specifically to do when you sit down tomorrow, and how to recognise the failure modes so you can climb out of them faster than I did.

Why slow drafting is the trap

The instinct is reasonable. You're building a thing — surely you should make each piece good before moving on. Slow drafting feels responsible. It also fails for three structural reasons:

You can't see the shape of a story you haven't written. The midpoint of your novel changes how chapter one should read. The ending changes how chapter seven should read. If you polish chapter one before chapter twenty-eight exists, you're polishing toward a target that's about to move. Most of that work will be thrown away. The slow draft burns labour.

You lose the thread. Novels live or die on the writer's belief in the version of the story they're holding in their head. When you spend a month polishing chapter three, the rest of the book — the part that's still vapour — gets blurry. By the time you return to chapter four, the cast feels like strangers. You either re-read everything (slow) or guess (worse).

The reward function is broken. Polishing chapter one for the seventeenth time gives you a small dopamine hit. Writing chapter ten cold gives you a much bigger one — but the small reward is reliable, the big reward isn't. Your brain learns the lesson and starts steering you toward the easier hit. You stop progressing without noticing.

An ugly first draft inverts all three. The shape becomes visible. The thread stays intact. The reward is finishing, not perfecting.

What "ugly" actually means

"Ugly" doesn't mean careless. It means deliberately permitting yourself to be bad at parts of the craft you'll fix later. Specifically:

  • Stage directions are fine. "She walked into the kitchen and made coffee" is legal in a first draft, even though we both know the published version will give her a specific coffee-making ritual that reveals character.
  • Placeholder names are fine. If you don't know what to call a minor character, type [WAITER] in brackets and keep going. Find/replace later.
  • Dialogue tags repeat. "Said" twelve times in a paragraph is fine. The variety pass comes later.
  • Research gaps are fine. Type [LOOK UP HOW THIS WORKS] in brackets and keep going. Don't let the desire to be accurate kill momentum.
  • Scenes you don't want to write are fine. Write a one-line scene summary in brackets and skip. Some of those scenes will turn out not to be needed at all by the time you reach the climax.
  • The opening sentence is fine. The version you write first is almost never the version you publish. Write something. Move on.

What "ugly" doesn't mean: skipping structure, inventing logic that breaks later, ignoring continuity. The skeleton has to hold up. The decoration is what gets thrown away. If your chapter four invalidates your chapter two, fix the contradiction in a one-line note and keep moving.

The brackets rule. If a sentence contains a square-bracketed [PLACEHOLDER], it's a future-you problem. Future-you is faster than present-you because future-you has the whole book in front of them. Trust the brackets. They're cheap, fast, and reliable.

The eight-to-twelve-week plan

For a 90,000-word novel, the maths is brutal but not heroic. Eight weeks is 1,610 words a day. Twelve weeks is 1,070 words a day. Both are achievable for a working writer with a job and a life — the bottleneck is consistency, not speed.

Here is the plan that produces an ugly complete draft most reliably:

Pre-week zero — outline (one weekend)

Spend a Saturday and a Sunday outlining. Three-act structure, eight beats, three sentences per beat. (We have a free template for exactly this.) Don't outline scenes yet — outline beats. The scenes will emerge.

Week one — first 10,000 words, no editing

Write your first 10,000 words without re-reading anything you've written. The goal is to install the habit. The words will be rough. That's the assignment.

One trick: at the end of each session, write a one-line note to yourself about what happens next. The next morning, start with that note instead of re-reading. You'll skip the polishing impulse entirely.

Weeks two through ten — 1,500 words a day

Same rule: don't re-read. If you discover something in chapter five that contradicts chapter two, write a one-line note in a "fix later" doc and keep going. The note will take you fifteen seconds. Going back to fix chapter two right now will take you a week and you won't recover.

Track word count daily. Use a streak counter — tools like InkSpaceX's daily goal chip exist for this and they work because they make the cost of skipping a day visible.

Weeks eleven and twelve — finish

The last 5,000 words are usually the hardest because the climax is in sight and you want it to be perfect. Resist. Get to the words "the end" and stop. Even if the climax feels weak. Even if you know the ending is wrong. Get to the end.

Then close the document for at least two weeks. Don't read it. Don't think about it. Work on something else. The book has to become a stranger before you can revise it well.

The three failure modes (and the fix for each)

Most writers who try this and abandon it fall into one of three traps. Knowing them in advance is half the cure.

The chapter-one loop. You finish chapter one, re-read it, decide it's too rough, rewrite it. By Sunday you've written 800 net words. Fix: ban yourself from re-reading any chapter you've finished until the whole draft is done. If you must edit something to keep moving, edit only the sentence you're about to follow.

The research black hole. A scene needs you to know how a 1923 telephone switchboard worked. You spend three hours on Wikipedia. Fix: [LOOK UP SWITCHBOARD] in brackets, keep writing. You can find out next month. Almost always you'll discover the scene didn't actually need that detail.

The "I lost the plot" panic. Around chapter twelve, the book stops feeling like a book. The cast is wrong, the stakes are off, the premise is dumb. You're convinced you have to start over. Fix: don't. Every draft does this around the midpoint. The fact that the book feels broken means you're discovering the real version of it. Write a one-line note about what you'd change if you were rewriting from scratch — and then keep writing the current version forward. The "real" version emerges from the ugly one, not from a clean restart.

What happens when the ugly draft is done

You have a finished thing. That alone changes the relationship. You're no longer a person trying to write a novel — you're a person revising one. The work in front of you is concrete: this scene drags, this chapter is missing, this character disappears for sixty pages. You can fix all of those because they're visible.

Most novelists who finish a first draft revise it three or four times before showing it to anyone. Each revision is faster than the draft because the bones already exist. You're a sculptor with a block now, not a sculptor with an empty room.

One last thing

The writers I know who finish books aren't the ones with the most talent. They're the ones who treat the first draft as raw material instead of a performance. They're slightly embarrassed by what they produce on a Tuesday. They write it anyway. Saturday's draft is better because Tuesday's draft existed.

Be slightly embarrassed by your work. Write it anyway. The book is closer than you think.
